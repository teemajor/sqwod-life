## The supplement in every second shake

Collagen is everywhere: in coffee creamers, protein bars, gummies, and the "joint support" tub your client bought after a sore knee. The pitch is simple and intuitive. Tendons, ligaments and cartilage are made of collagen, so eating collagen should build them. Add vitamin C, take it an hour before training, and you're supposedly bulletproofing your joints.

Intuitive is not the same as proven. So we did the slow, useful thing: pulled the meta-analyses and the best controlled trials on joint pain, tendons, muscle and the timing protocol, graded them, and checked the registry for what's still open. The short version: there is a real but modest signal for joint pain, a genuinely split verdict on tendons, and a clear answer on muscle that the marketing tends to skip.

## Joint pain: collagen's best case (moderate evidence, modest effect)

Start here, because this is where the evidence is most consistent. A systematic review of 15 randomized trials combining collagen with exercise concluded that collagen was "most beneficial in improving joint functionality and reducing joint pain."<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup>

The two trials closest to your clients both used active people, not patients. In a 24-week trial at Penn State, varsity and club athletes with activity-related joint pain took 10 g of collagen hydrolysate a day or placebo. The collagen group improved more on several pain measures, including pain when walking, standing, carrying and lifting, with a stronger difference in the knee-pain subgroup. Only 97 of 147 starters could be analysed, and the authors themselves flag the study's limitations.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup> A second trial gave 139 athletes with functional knee pain 5 g of collagen peptides or placebo for 12 weeks. Activity-related pain fell by 19.5 points on a visual analogue scale with collagen versus 13.9 with placebo, a statistically significant gap, confirmed by the physicians' assessments. Pain at rest did not differ significantly.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup>

Read those numbers carefully. The placebo group improved by more than two thirds as much as the collagen group. That tells you most of the improvement a client feels on collagen would likely have happened anyway, and the extra slice is real but small.

In clinical osteoarthritis the signal holds up across pooled trials. A 2019 meta-analysis found collagen reduced total WOMAC osteoarthritis scores and pain on a visual analogue scale, although the WOMAC pain subscore alone did not differ significantly.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup> An updated 2025 meta-analysis of 11 RCTs and 870 people with knee osteoarthritis found significant improvements in both pain and function, but with very high variability between trials (I² of 88% for pain).<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup> High variability means trials disagree a lot on how big the effect is, so treat the average with caution.

**Evidence grade: moderate.** Consistent direction across RCTs and meta-analyses, modest size, big placebo responses and big between-trial variability. A reasonable thing for a client with activity-related joint pain to try, not a fix.

## Tendons and ligaments: the split verdict (weak and conflicting evidence)

This is where the marketing is loudest and the data is least settled.

The best pooled estimate comes from a 2024 meta-analysis of 19 RCTs and 768 participants combining collagen with long-term training. It found a moderate effect on tendon size (SMD 0.67), but rated the certainty of that evidence "very low" using the GRADE system.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-6">6</a></sup>

Look inside and you see why. Two trials from the same research group gave young active men 5 g a day of a specific collagen peptide product during 14 weeks of heavy lower-body training. In the first, Achilles tendon cross-sectional area grew +11.0% with collagen versus +4.7% with placebo, with extra calf muscle thickness too; tendon stiffness and strength improved equally in both groups.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-7">7</a></sup> The second found greater patellar tendon growth in parts of the tendon, again with no difference in stiffness, muscle size or strength.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-8">8</a></sup>

Then a separate trial gave 39 young men a higher dose, 15 g a day, through 15 weeks of lower-body training, and measured tendon size by MRI. Result: no between-group difference in any tendon outcome. Both groups gained patellar tendon stiffness from training alone (+17.3% with collagen, +20.9% with placebo).<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup> A bigger dose, a well-controlled design, and nothing extra.

Notice the pattern across all three: even in the positive trials, the parts of tendon function a lifter actually cares about, stiffness and strength, improved the same with or without collagen. The training did that.

For injured tendons the evidence is even thinner. A 20-person pilot crossover trial in chronic Achilles tendinopathy found faster improvement in symptom scores when branded collagen peptides were added to a calf-loading program.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-10">10</a></sup> Interesting, small, and a pilot.

**Evidence grade: weak and conflicting.** Tendon size may grow a little more with collagen; tendon stiffness and strength don't seem to. No trial we graded measured whether collagen actually reduces tendon or ligament injuries.

## The vitamin C and timing protocol (what's measured vs. what's claimed)

The popular protocol, collagen or gelatin plus vitamin C about an hour before a short loading session, traces back to a 2017 study from the Australian Institute of Sport. Eight men took vitamin C-enriched gelatin (5 or 15 g) or placebo before six minutes of rope skipping. With 15 g, a blood marker of collagen production (PINP) roughly doubled.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-11">11</a></sup>

That's a real, plausible mechanism. But it's a blood marker in eight people, not tendon strength, and not injuries. And the follow-up work doesn't fully agree on dose. In a 2024 crossover trial, 10 resistance-trained men took 0, 15 or 30 g of hydrolysed collagen with 50 mg of vitamin C an hour before heavy squats. Only 30 g raised the collagen synthesis marker more than placebo; 15 g was no different from nothing.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-12">12</a></sup>

Inside the muscle itself, the picture is flatter still. A Maastricht trial of 45 recreational athletes found that 30 g of collagen after resistance exercise did not significantly increase muscle connective protein synthesis compared with placebo.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-13">13</a></sup>

One more honest point: in the protocols above, vitamin C came bundled in with the collagen. None of these trials tested collagen with versus without vitamin C, so the specific claim that vitamin C "activates" your collagen dose has not been isolated in the studies we graded.

**Evidence grade: weak.** Pre-training collagen can raise a blood marker of collagen production, at least at higher doses. Whether that turns into stronger tendons or fewer injuries is unproven.

## Muscle: collagen is the wrong protein for the job (strong evidence)

Here the evidence is clear, and it's the part to get right with clients. After a dose, collagen puts far less leucine and essential amino acids into the blood than whey does,<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-13">13</a></sup> and controlled trials show what that means for muscle.

In older women, 30 g of whey twice daily raised muscle protein synthesis both acutely and over six days; the same amount of collagen did not raise longer-term muscle protein synthesis above baseline, with or without exercise.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-14">14</a></sup> In young recreational athletes, only whey significantly raised post-exercise muscle-building rates compared with placebo; collagen did not.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-13">13</a></sup>

So what about the trial where collagen plus lifting built more muscle? In 53 older men with sarcopenia, 15 g of collagen after training led to more fat-free mass gain (+4.2 kg vs. +2.9 kg) and more strength than placebo over 12 weeks.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-15">15</a></sup> But the placebo was silica, which contains no protein at all. That trial shows extra protein helps undernourished older lifters; it doesn't show collagen beats a better protein. The 2024 meta-analysis did find a small effect on maximal strength (SMD 0.19, low certainty) and a moderate-certainty effect on fat-free mass (SMD 0.48) in trials comparing collagen to placebo.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-6">6</a></sup> Read that as "some protein beats no protein", not as a reason to choose collagen.

**Evidence grade: strong for "inferior to whey and other complete proteins for building muscle."** Collagen grams should not count toward a client's protein target as if they were whey, meat, fish, eggs or soy.

## What's about to be tested (the part nobody else shows you)

The clinical registry currently lists **9 trials** recruiting or about to start that test oral collagen on tendon, joint, muscle or exercise outcomes.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-16">16</a></sup> A few are worth watching.

The most useful for the dose question: a **45-person, three-arm trial** (NCT07800780) comparing 10 g hydrolysed collagen, 5 g of branded bioactive collagen peptides and placebo for knee osteoarthritis pain and function, with primary completion in May 2027. A **University of Arkansas trial** (NCT05823727) is testing collagen peptides on connective tissue remodelling and function after knee replacement. A **UC Davis study** (NCT06138106) is developing a supplement specifically to increase collagen synthesis in people. And a **Loughborough University trial** (NCT07740967) is testing collagen plus omega-3 in middle-aged women, with joint pain and muscle strength among the outcomes.

Notice what's missing: none of these is a large trial asking whether collagen prevents tendon or ligament injuries in healthy athletes. That's the claim on most labels, and it's still the one nobody is testing at scale.

## Your script for Monday

When the next client asks whether they should be taking collagen for their joints, here's the whole thing in plain language:

*"Short answer: it might help a little with achy joints, but it won't build your tendons for you, and it doesn't count as protein for muscle. The best studies in active people found a modest drop in joint pain over about three months, and people on the dummy powder got better too. On tendons the research is split: some studies show a bit more tendon growth, one bigger study showed nothing extra, and in all of them the training did the real work. If you want to try it, take 5 to 10 grams a day for 12 weeks, keep a quick pain log, and we'll check whether it's worth the money. Your protein still comes from real protein: dairy, meat, fish, eggs or a whey shake. And if a joint is swollen or the pain doesn't settle, that's a physio or doctor visit, not a supplement."*

That's it. No bulletproof-joints promise, no stopwatch-timed gelatin ritual, no swapping whey for collagen. Just the evidence, translated into something you can use with a client, which is the whole job.
