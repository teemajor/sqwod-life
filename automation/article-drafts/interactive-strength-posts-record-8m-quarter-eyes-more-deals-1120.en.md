## Interactive Strength's roll-up hits a new revenue record

Interactive Strength Inc. (Nasdaq: TRNR), the parent company of Wattbike, Ergatta, Clmbr and Forme, reported record third-quarter revenue of $8 million, up 66% year over year, according to [Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/interactive-strength-trnr-grows-revenue-following-acquisitions-eyes-more-deals/). Gross margin improved sharply to 41%, compared with 17% a year earlier, and the company's adjusted EBITDA loss narrowed to approximately $400,000, down from $2.9 million in the same period last year. CEO Trent Ward described Q3 2026 as "the second consecutive quarter in which the earnings power of our M&A-driven operating strategy is showing in our reported results." The company also expects to close its acquisition of Stepr before year-end, a deal the Sqwod Deal Tracker recorded in July 2026 at $6.7 million plus earn-outs, adding a stair-climbing machine brand to an already broad connected-equipment portfolio.

## What a consolidating equipment market means for operators

When a single publicly traded company controls Wattbike, Ergatta, Clmbr and soon Stepr, the competitive dynamics for independent studios shift. Pricing, software subscriptions and hardware support all sit inside one entity with its own margin targets. For operators who have built programming or branding around any of these products, vendor consolidation reduces negotiating leverage and raises the question of long-term product support priorities. The broader consolidation trend is not limited to equipment. The Sqwod Deal Tracker has recorded more than 30 gym and studio deals since January 2026, with disclosed values totalling $1.6 billion across names including Equinox, PureGym and FitLab. Interactive Strength's moves are happening inside an industry that is consolidating at multiple levels simultaneously, from large club chains to connected hardware.

## Sqwod's take: the roll-up model rewards patience, not smaller operators

Interactive Strength's improving numbers are a genuine milestone for a company that was losing nearly $6 million at the operating line a year ago. The trajectory is clear and the strategy is coherent. But the winners here are Interactive Strength's shareholders, not the studio owner who bought an Ergatta rower or a Clmbr unit expecting an independent brand relationship. As more connected equipment brands fold into holding companies chasing adjusted profitability targets, operators should treat hardware vendors the way they treat any consolidating supplier: document your contracts carefully, understand what happens to software access if a brand is restructured, and avoid building core programming around a single piece of connected equipment whose roadmap you do not control. The company has not yet reached its stated goal of adjusted profitability by end of 2026, and further acquisitions are explicitly on the table. More deals mean more integration risk, and that risk sits upstream of every operator who relies on these products on the gym floor.
