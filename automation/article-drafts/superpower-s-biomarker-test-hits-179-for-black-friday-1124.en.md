## Superpower cuts its membership price as biomarker testing goes mainstream

Superpower, a telehealth company founded in 2023, is selling its annual membership for $179 this Black Friday, down from $199, according to [Athletech News](https://athletechnews.com/superpower-blood-test-review/). The membership covers a blood draw at one of more than 3,000 partner labs nationwide, including Quest Diagnostics, plus an option for in-home collection at an extra fee. Members receive a full report covering more than 100 biomarkers, 17 health scores, a personalized action plan, and unlimited access to an AI-powered health concierge. The company says its testing goes five times deeper than standard bloodwork and screens for early signs of more than 1,000 conditions. The membership is not covered by insurance but is HSA- and FSA-eligible.

## Why fitness operators should pay attention to consumer health data

Biomarker services like Superpower sit directly in the space fitness operators are already trying to own: personalized, evidence-based wellness. When a client arrives at a studio or gym holding a report with 17 scored health categories and an AI action plan, the conversation shifts. Coaches either become a delivery mechanism for that plan or risk being bypassed by it. The Sqwod Deal Tracker shows strong investor appetite in this adjacent space: nutrition and supplements deals alone account for more than $7.1 billion in disclosed funding across 15 tracked transactions, with recent raises including Persona at $10 million and Nua at $50 million. Consumer willingness to spend on health data is not slowing. At $179 a year, Superpower is priced within reach of the same demographic that pays for boutique fitness memberships, which means adoption among existing gym clients is a realistic near-term scenario, not a distant trend.

## Superpower hands operators a choice, not just a tool

The more interesting question is not whether Superpower's test is worth $179. Athletech News found it worth reviewing seriously, and the product features are straightforward. The harder question is what a studio or operator does the morning a client walks in with Superpower results in hand. There are two plausible outcomes. The first is friction: a coach who cannot read or contextualize biomarker data looks underprepared next to an AI concierge that already issued a plan. The second is leverage: an operator who trains staff to work alongside these reports, or formally partners with services like this, turns a potential disruption into a retention tool. The AI concierge tells a client what to do in general; the coach is the person in the room who makes it happen. That is still a durable advantage, but only if operators act on it deliberately. Services priced at $179 with FSA eligibility will find their audience. The studios that figure out how to meet that audience with something the AI cannot provide are the ones that come out ahead.
