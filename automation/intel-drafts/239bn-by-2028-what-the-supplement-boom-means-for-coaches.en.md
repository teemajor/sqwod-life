## The Size of the Opportunity (And Why Most Coaches Are Missing It)

The global dietary supplement market was **$158bn in 2023** and is forecast to reach $239.4bn by 2028 (Adroit Market Research via Statista). Look at Chart 1. That is not a spike. It is a straight line up, from $112.3bn in 2018 to a forecast that compounds consistently across a full decade. No single year in that series shows a contraction. This is structural growth, not a trend.

Alongside it, the functional food and beverage market was **$281.14bn in 2021** and is forecast at $529.66bn by 2028 (Fortune Business Insights via Statista). See Chart 2. Combined, these two categories represent more than $750bn in annual revenue by the end of this decade. That is larger than the GDP of the Netherlands, Switzerland, or Saudi Arabia.

Coaches are not operating at the edge of this market. They are sitting at its centre. Every client conversation about nutrition, recovery, energy, and health is a moment inside this market. The problem is that most coaches have no structured strategy attached to that position.

The gap is not knowledge. Most coaches know that protein matters, that many clients take Vitamin D, that gut health is a growing conversation. The gap is commercial architecture. There is no documented recommendation protocol, no supplier relationship, no affiliate or resale model, and no tracking of which clients engage with supplements and which do not.

From 2018 to 2023, the market added **$45.7bn in annual revenue** (from $112.3bn to $158bn) (Adroit Market Research via Statista). From 2023 to 2028, it is forecast to add another $81.4bn. The second wave is nearly twice the size of the first. Coaches who build a supplement strategy now are building into the larger half of the growth curve.

This is not about becoming a retailer. It is about being the trusted, informed voice your client turns to when they are already going to spend money. The average supplement consumer is not asking whether to buy. They are asking what to buy. If you are not answering that question, someone else is.

## Who Is Buying and Why They Buy

Start with who is actually purchasing supplements. The 2019 Glanbia survey of 2,257 supplement consumers across the US, UK, and China found that **46% use Vitamin C** and 41% use Vitamin D (Glanbia via NutraIngredients-USA.com). See Chart 7. These are not niche performance products. They are the most widely distributed consumer health purchases in three of the world's largest economies.

Further down the same chart: 34% use Calcium, 32% use B Vitamins, 29% use Iron. The top five products are all foundational health supplements. Performance products ,  creatine, pre-workout, specific sports nutrition ,  do not appear in the top ten. The supplement consumer is not, in the majority, a gym enthusiast chasing a performance edge. They are a general health consumer looking for basic coverage.

The purchase motivation data confirms this. **43% of supplement consumers cite general health** as their primary reason for taking supplements (Glanbia via NutraIngredients-USA.com). See Chart 8. Heart health is second at 31%. Healthy aging is third at 25%. Mental and cognitive health sits at 24%. Joint health is at 24%. Digestive health is at 23%.

Muscle health, the category coaches most often default to when talking about supplements, sits at **16%** (Glanbia via NutraIngredients-USA.com). It is the tenth most cited motivation out of thirteen listed. If a coach only speaks about supplements in the language of performance, gains, and muscle, they are speaking to the smallest segment of the buying population.

This has direct practical implications. A client who is a 48-year-old woman managing her energy levels, bone density, and stress response is not going to engage with a recommendation framed around muscle protein synthesis. The same recommendation, framed around general health, bone health, and cognitive support, lands with the majority of your client base rather than a fraction of it.

The persona that emerges from the data is specific. Your typical supplement-buying client is a mainstream health consumer. They are not an outlier. They are already spending. They already have products on their kitchen counter. They want guidance they can trust from someone who knows their body, their goals, and their history. That person is you, if you choose to be.

The 23% who already use probiotics (Glanbia via NutraIngredients-USA.com) are particularly significant. They have already moved beyond the basics. They represent clients who are open to more specific, category-led recommendations. They are the early adopters inside your existing client base.

## The Regional Landscape: Where the Money Is

The supplement market is global but it is not evenly distributed. Chart 6 shows the regional breakdown. **North America leads at a forecast $72.06bn by 2026**, up from $42.56bn in 2018 (Reports And Data via Statista). That is growth of $29.5bn in eight years from a single region.

Asia-Pacific follows at a forecast **$52.07bn by 2026**, up from $31.28bn in 2018 (Reports And Data via Statista). Europe is forecast at $47.71bn by 2026, up from $25.93bn in 2018. All three regions are growing. None are contracting.

Chart 10 gives the country-level picture for vitamins and minerals specifically. **China led with $4.82bn in revenue in 2023** (Statista Consumer Market Insights). The United States was second at $4.37bn. India was third at $2.40bn. After that: Australia at $1.30bn, Brazil at $1.18bn, Japan at $1.02bn.

India's position is notable. At $2.40bn (Statista Consumer Market Insights), it sits third globally in vitamins and minerals revenue, ahead of Australia, Brazil, Japan, and Canada. For coaches and operators building businesses in India, this is not a frontier market. It is already the third-largest vitamins and minerals market in the world.

The practical read for operators is straightforward. If you are based in North America, you are operating in the largest regional market but also the most competitive one. Supplier relationships are mature, consumer awareness is high, and your clients have access to every product you might recommend through Amazon, Walmart, and direct-to-consumer brands. The opportunity is in curation and trust, not access.

If you are based in Asia-Pacific or in an emerging market within that region, the growth curve is steeper. Revenue per capita is lower, meaning there is more room for the market to expand. The consumer base is less saturated with direct-to-consumer supplement marketing, which means a trusted coach's recommendation carries relatively more weight.

For operators in Europe, the regulatory environment is more complex than North America, particularly around health claims. That is not a reason to avoid the category. It is a reason to build your recommendation protocols carefully, with claim-compliant language and supplier documentation to match.

Regional context does not change the strategic logic. It changes the execution. The core position remains the same everywhere: coaches are trusted, clients are already buying, and the question is whether you have a structured role in that transaction.

## The Vitamins and Minerals Engine

Chart 3 is the clearest signal in this report for coaches who want to know where to start. Vitamins and minerals revenue grew from **$23.56bn in 2020 to $29.7bn in 2023** (Statista Consumer Market Insights). That is $6.14bn of additional annual revenue in three years, in one category.

The growth rate in 2023 alone was **8.01%** (Statista Consumer Market Insights). See Chart 5. In a category that large, 8% annual growth is not a sign of maturation. It is a sign of persistent, broad-based demand. The segment is forecast to reach $44.18bn by 2029 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). That trajectory holds above 6% annually through the end of the decade.

Revenue per capita gives a different but equally important lens. Global vitamins and minerals spend per person reached **$3.86 in 2023** and is forecast at $5.48 by 2029 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). The per-capita figure matters for coaches because it normalises the opportunity across markets of different sizes. Even in countries where the total market is modest, individuals are spending more each year. The per-person spend is rising almost everywhere.

For coaches choosing where to focus first, vitamins and minerals is the correct answer for three reasons. First, client familiarity is the highest of any supplement category. Every client has heard of Vitamin C, Vitamin D, and Calcium. No education barrier exists. Second, regulatory complexity is lower than categories like sports nutrition or herbal products in most markets. The products are well understood by regulators and the label requirements are established. Third, supplier availability is the broadest. From pharmaceutical-grade manufacturers to well-known consumer brands, options exist at every price point.

The practical entry point is not complicated. Pick the two most commonly used products in the category, which the survey data identifies clearly, and build a documented recommendation around them. Write down which clients you would recommend each product to. Write down the rationale. Write down what you would tell them to look for on a label. That document is the beginning of a supplement recommendation protocol, and it costs nothing to create.

The vitamins and minerals segment is not exciting in the way that newer categories like adaptogens or nootropics are. That is precisely why it is the right starting point. It is large, it is growing, it is familiar to clients, and it is the category in which you can build trust without needing to educate your client from scratch.

## Probiotics: The Fastest-Growing Segment a Coach Can Own

The probiotic supplements market grew from **$1.22bn in 2017 to a forecast $3.28bn by 2027** (Netscribes). See Chart 4. That is growth of more than 2.6 times across a decade. No other segment covered in this report shows that rate of multiplication at that scale.

The growth is not speculative. Every year in the series shows positive movement. From $1.22bn in 2017 to $2.50bn in 2023, the market has already more than doubled before the forecast period ends (Netscribes). The 2027 figure is a continuation of a trend already in progress, not a projection built on assumption.

Understanding where that money goes within the category matters for coaches. Chart 9 shows the application breakdown. **Gastrointestinal health is the dominant application at a forecast $2,095.4m by 2027** (Netscribes), up from $732.2m in 2017. Immune health is second at a forecast $636.2m by 2027. Vaginal health, urinary tract health, and oral health follow. The gut-health framing is where the volume is.

For coaches, this aligns with something clients are already talking about. Bloating, digestion, energy, immunity. These conversations happen in studios, in gyms, and in online check-ins every week. The supplement that addresses all of those entry points in one product is a probiotic. The coach who can connect a client's complaint to a product category and explain why it is relevant has a meaningful, trust-based role in that purchase decision.

Format matters for practical stocking decisions. **Capsules are the leading format at a forecast $1,467.7m by 2027** (Netscribes), ahead of powder at $555.6m and chewables at $387.2m. Capsules are shelf-stable, easy to transport, familiar to consumers, and simple to recommend. If you are adding a probiotic to your offer, capsule format is the obvious starting point.

Online probiotic supplement sales reached **$700.3m in 2023** and are forecast at $955m by 2027 (Netscribes). That channel data validates an affiliate or referral model. If your client base is partially or fully online, the purchasing infrastructure already exists. You recommend, they click, they buy. The mechanics of a digital supplement recommendation strategy are already established in the market.

The probiotic opportunity for coaches is specific: it sits at the intersection of a fast-growing category, a broadly relatable client need (gut and immune health), and a format that is easy to stock or refer. No other supplement segment gives coaches that combination as clearly.

## Supply Chain: The Risk Most Coaches Do Not See

Most coaches think about supplements from the front of the bottle. The ingredient name, the dose, the brand. Very few think about where the raw material was manufactured, by whom, and how concentrated that supply chain is. That gap is a risk they are passing on to their clients without knowing it.

**China exported 341.93 million kg of vitamins in 2022** (UN Comtrade). The next largest exporter was the Netherlands at 70.09 million kg. The gap between first and second place is 271.84 million kg. No other exporter is within four times of China's volume. The Netherlands, United States, Australia, Germany, India, France, Belgium, Switzerland, Spain, and South Africa make up the rest of the top eleven, and their combined volumes do not match China's figure.

On export value, **China led at $3.70bn in 2021**, with the United States second at $1.02bn (UN Comtrade). France was third at $460m, Germany fourth at $448m, and the Netherlands fifth at $423m. The value gap between China and the United States alone is $2.68bn.

The import side of the equation is equally concentrated. **The United States was the largest vitamin importer by value in 2022 at $1,556m** (UN Comtrade). Germany was second at $751m, the Netherlands third at $518m. A US-based coach whose clients are buying American-branded supplements is, in the majority of cases, operating inside a supply chain that begins in China. The brand on the label is American. The ingredient is not necessarily.

This is not an argument against Chinese manufacturing. It is an argument for knowing your supply chain. Country-of-origin transparency is increasingly a consumer expectation, particularly among health-conscious clients who are already reading labels. A coach who understands where raw materials come from, who can tell a client whether a supplier discloses their manufacturing origin, and who has made a deliberate choice based on that information is in a different category of credibility from one who simply recommends whatever brand is popular.

The practical move is straightforward. When you evaluate a supplement supplier, ask specifically about manufacturing origin for active ingredients. Look for suppliers who publish Certificates of Analysis from third-party labs. If a supplier cannot tell you where their raw materials come from, that is information worth having before you recommend their product to a client.

Supply chain concentration is also a business continuity risk. Any disruption to Chinese vitamin manufacturing, whether from regulatory action, raw material shortages, or trade policy, moves through the global supplement market quickly. Coaches who are aware of this can make more resilient choices about which suppliers to work with.

## What the Big Players Signal About Where the Category Is Going

When large companies allocate significant portions of their revenue to nutrition, it is not a branding decision. It is a capital allocation decision. It signals that their research, their sales data, and their forecasting models all point in the same direction.

Abbott Laboratories is one of the clearest signals in the data. Their **International Adult Nutritionals revenue grew from $2,017m in 2019 to $2,784m in 2023** (Abbott Laboratories). That is growth of $767m, or 38%, in four years, in one sub-category of one company's portfolio. Adult nutrition, not pediatric, not pharmaceutical, not diagnostics. The adult nutrition segment is the growth engine inside Abbott's business, and it has grown through a pandemic, supply chain disruptions, and inflationary pressure.

Herbalife's category split shows where consumer spending is concentrated. **Weight management products represent 56.8% of Herbalife's net sales** (Herbalife). Targeted nutrition is 29.1%. Together, those two categories account for 87.9% of the company's global sales. Energy, sports, and fitness is 10.6%. The two categories that sit at 85.9% of revenue are precisely the categories coaches most directly influence in client conversations.

Otsuka Holdings provides a pharma-adjacent data point. Their nutraceutical business represents **25.1% of total group revenue** (Otsuka Holdings). A company that also runs a pharmaceutical business with 65.5% of revenue has allocated one quarter of its total revenue to nutraceuticals. That is not a side bet. That is a core business line.

Bayer's Consumer Health segment, which includes vitamins and over-the-counter health products, held **12.7% of net sales in 2023** (Bayer). Bayer's primary businesses are Crop Science and Pharmaceuticals. The fact that consumer health, which is the category closest to supplement retail, holds more than one in eight dollars of revenue at a company of that scale indicates sustained consumer demand.

The signal from large companies is consistent. Adult nutrition is growing. Weight management and targeted nutrition are the dominant consumer categories. Pharma-adjacent companies are treating nutraceuticals as a core revenue line, not an experiment. Coaches who build their practice around these categories are aligned with where institutional capital, product development, and supply chain investment are all pointing.

## The Functional Food Adjacency Coaches Are Ignoring

Most coaches think about supplements as a separate category from food. A capsule is a supplement. A meal is food. The market does not draw that line the same way.

The functional food and beverage market was **$281.14bn in 2021** and is forecast at $529.66bn by 2028 (Fortune Business Insights via Statista). See Chart 2. From $281.14bn to $529.66bn is an increase of $248.52bn. The supplement market's entire projected 2028 size is $239.4bn. Functional food and beverage will add more than the entire supplement market's value in incremental growth over the same period.

A parallel dataset from Allied Market Research puts the functional food market at **$177.77bn in 2019**, forecast at $267.92bn by 2027 (Allied Market Research via Statista). Different methodology, different scope, consistent direction.

Within functional food, two sub-categories are directly relevant to coaches. Functional protein is a **$6.53bn market in 2023**, forecast at $7.98bn by 2026 (Fior Markets via Statista). Dietary fibers were **$6.86bn in 2023** (Statista via Verified Market Research), forecast at $10.22bn by 2027. Both of these are categories that coaches already talk about every day in the context of nutrition coaching, meal planning, and body composition.

The difference between a coach who recommends a protein supplement and a coach who understands functional protein as a $6.53bn market with specific product segments is the difference between giving ad hoc advice and operating inside a structured commercial category. The knowledge base required to do the latter is not much larger. The positioning shift is significant.

Functional food extends the coaching touchpoint beyond the gym session and beyond the capsule. A client who takes a Vitamin D supplement interacts with that recommendation once a day. A client whose food choices are shaped by a coach's functional food guidance interacts with that guidance at every meal. The frequency of influence is fundamentally different.

Coaches who move into functional food recommendations are not overreaching. They are responding to where their clients already are. The client who is buying protein bars, fortified plant milks, prebiotic fiber drinks, and omega-3 enriched eggs is already inside the functional food market. The question is whether they have a knowledgeable guide helping them make those choices, or whether they are making them based on packaging copy and social media.

## What to Do: A Five-Move Operator Playbook

The market data is clear. The question is what to do with it this month, not in the abstract.

**Move one: build a Vitamin C and Vitamin D recommendation protocol.**

These are the two most commonly used supplements among consumers across the US, UK, and China, at 46% and 41% respectively (Glanbia via NutraIngredients-USA.com). Your clients are likely already using them or have used them. The opportunity is not to introduce them to the products. It is to become the credible voice on quality, dose, and form. Document which clients you would recommend each product to. Write down three things you would tell a client to look for on the label. That is a protocol. It takes one hour to create and it is the foundation of everything else.

**Move two: reframe your supplement language around health, not performance.**

43% of supplement consumers cite general health as their primary motivation and 31% cite heart health (Glanbia via NutraIngredients-USA.com). Only 16% cite muscle health. If your default supplement conversation uses performance language, you are speaking to the smallest segment of your client base. Rewrite your client-facing language. Lead with energy, immunity, general wellness, and healthy aging. Keep performance framing for the clients for whom it is genuinely the right frame.

**Move three: add a gut-health probiotic capsule recommendation to your offer.**

The gastrointestinal health application of probiotics is forecast at $2,095.4m by 2027 (Netscribes). The capsule format leads at $1,467.7m (Netscribes) and is the easiest to recommend and resell. Your clients are already talking about gut health. Give them a specific, documented recommendation backed by your rationale. Whether you resell, affiliate-refer, or simply advise, the act of making a specific recommendation in this category positions you as a knowledgeable practitioner, not a generalist.

**Move four: audit your supply chain for country-of-origin concentration.**

China exported 341.93 million kg of vitamins in 2022, more than four times the next largest exporter (UN Comtrade). The US imported $1,556m in vitamins in 2022 (UN Comtrade), most of which traces back through this supply chain. Contact your current or prospective supplement suppliers and ask one question: where are the active ingredients in this product manufactured? If they cannot answer, find a supplier who can. Make this a client-facing argument. Transparency on origin is a credibility asset.

**Move five: track your supplement-engaged clients as a distinct cohort.**

The vitamins and minerals segment grew **8.01% in 2023** (Statista Consumer Market Insights). The general fitness market does not grow at that rate. The clients in your base who are actively engaged with supplements are the fastest-growing consumer segment in the wellness economy. They are spending more, they are more engaged with their health, and they represent the highest-value client profile in most fitness businesses. Know who they are. Track their engagement, their retention, and their referral behaviour separately. That cohort tells you more about the future of your business than your average client does.

Three actions to take this month:

Write your two-product Vitamin C and Vitamin D recommendation protocol before the end of the week. One page. Rationale, dosing context, label criteria.

Email or message five current clients who you know are already taking supplements. Ask them what they are taking and why. The answers will tell you exactly where the recommendation gaps are in your existing client base.

Request country-of-origin documentation from one supplement supplier you currently use or are considering. The process of asking will sharpen your supplier criteria faster than any amount of desk research.
