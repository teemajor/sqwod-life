> **How we sourced this.** Every number below links to its source. Where a figure comes from a peer-reviewed study, we say so. Where it comes from a software vendor's own platform data or a trade write-up, we say that too, because those are useful but not independent.

## Members leave long before they cancel

The cancellation email is the last step, not the first. In a study of 7,752 members at three US health clubs (data from 1997 to 2001), monthly members let **2.3 months on average** pass between their last visit and the end of their contract.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup> In a 2026 survey of 1,393 current and former US gym members by software vendor Zenoti, **53% said they had stopped going at some point without ever formally cancelling**, and nearly half of lapsed members said their gym never reached out.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup> Platform data from booking software shows behaviour changing **3 to 8 months** before a cancel or downgrade.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup>

That gap is your window. Billing keeps running, so the dashboard looks fine, while the member is already gone in their head.

How big is the problem? The Health & Fitness Association's 2025 benchmark puts average annual member retention at **66.4%**, across 175 companies and more than 17,000 facilities.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup> A 2013 UK report on 342,759 memberships (joined 2009 to 2012) found that only **51.9%** of members stay at least 12 months.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup> The first months matter most: in one study of 5,240 members at a single gym in Brazil, 63% stopped before the third month.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-6">6</a></sup> That gym is not a benchmark for yours, but the shape repeats across the research: early months decide a lot.

## Sign 1: days since the last visit

This is the strongest signal we found. A study of 5,209 members of a fitness club in Lisbon tested several prediction models, and **days without a visit carried 35 to 54% of the predictive weight**, ahead of how long someone had been a member and how much they paid.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-7">7</a></sup> One of its decision rules flagged members after just **7.5 days** without a visit, combined with age and spend.

A larger study of 14,522 members across three Spanish centres predicted dropout with about 70% accuracy from monthly behaviour data, but the best model differed from centre to centre.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-8">8</a></sup> The lesson for an operator: use your own numbers. A gap that is normal for a twice-a-week member is an alarm for a five-times-a-week member.

## Sign 2: visits are falling, slowly

Members rarely stop overnight. In booking-platform data, members who later cancelled went from **6.2 to 5.4 visits a month** over their last three months and spent 11% less. Long-standing members who downgraded dropped from **7.4 to 4.5 visits** (−39%) and spent 24% less first.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup> This is vendor data and shows a pattern, not cause and effect, but it matches what the academic studies find about frequency.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-7">7</a>,&#8202;<a href="#source-8">8</a></sup>

Compare each member with their own recent average. A drop of a quarter or more is worth a conversation.

## Sign 3: a slow start

Research by Melvyn Hillsdon (University of Exeter) and retention researcher Paul Bedford, replicated across more than three million member records, names visit frequency, staff interaction, contract length, price and age as the stable predictors. The same work reports that a **first visit more than two weeks after joining** means much higher risk, that members with a fixed weekly routine stay about six months longer, and that members who made a friend at the club in the last three months were **40% less likely to cancel**.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-9">9</a></sup> These figures come from a trade-magazine summary, not a peer-reviewed paper, so treat them as strong practitioner evidence.

In practice: book the second and third visit before the member leaves the first one, and introduce them to at least one other member or coach by name.

## Sign 4: a failed payment

Not all churn is a decision. When a card fails and nobody follows up, you lose a member who wanted to stay. Across subscription businesses, Recurly's 2026 benchmarks put involuntary churn at **1.25%** against 2.34% voluntary, so roughly a third of all churn.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-11">11</a></sup> Stripe reported that nearly **a quarter of churn** comes from missed payments or declined cards.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-12">12</a></sup> ABC Fitness says roughly **7 to 12% of monthly dues transactions** fail in its billing data.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-13">13</a></sup> All three are vendor figures, but they point the same way, and this is the cheapest churn to fix: automatic card updates, retries on the right days, and a friendly message with a one-tap link.

## What actually brings them back

**People, more than messages.** In a randomised trial with 2,463 gym members, twelve weekly reminder emails raised visits by about 13%, and the effect partly persisted for three months afterwards, but **did not change how long people stayed or whether they renewed**.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-14">14</a></sup> A megastudy of 61,293 members of a US gym chain tested 54 four-week programmes: 45% of them raised weekly visits by 9 to 27%, but only 8% had effects that lasted after the programme ended.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-15">15</a></sup> Automated nudges help with visits. They are not a retention strategy on their own.

What the research links to fewer cancellations is the conversation. In data on 13,722 members, a high-risk visit carried about a 5% chance of cancelling the following month. When a staff interaction at that visit was followed by a repeat visit within seven days, next-month cancellation risk was **86% lower** than with no interaction. This is an observational trade-magazine analysis, and the 'effective' group is defined by the member coming back, so it overstates what a conversation alone achieves. The author estimates one cancellation prevented for every 12 such interactions.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-10">10</a></sup>

**Lapsed is not lost.** In the long-term study of one gym in Brazil, 38% of members who dropped out came back within a year, more than half of them within the first month.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-16">16</a></sup> A personal "we noticed you've been away" in the first weeks reaches people while they still think of themselves as members.

## A note for studios in Germany

Since 1 March 2022, standard-form consumer contracts in Germany can still bind for at most two years at first, but may then only roll on indefinitely, cancellable at any time with at most one month's notice.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-17">17</a></sup> Since July 2022, if members can sign up on your website, that website also needs a cancellation button.<sup class="fn"><a href="#source-18">18</a></sup> We found no data on how this changed cancellation rates. But it means a long minimum term protects you less than it used to, so spotting at-risk members early matters even more.

## Your weekly churn check

1. **List members with no visit in 7+ days** (first three months) or **14+ days** (everyone else).
2. **List members whose monthly visits fell by a quarter or more** against their own last three months.
3. **List this week's failed payments** and fix them the same day.
4. **Give every name a personal contact** from a coach or the front desk. Ask how they are, not when they are coming back.
5. **Track two numbers:** how many you contacted, and how many visited again within 14 days.

The thresholds are starting points. After eight weeks, check which signal actually came before your cancellations, and move the lines to match your own members.
