## The Intent-Action Gap: Why 60% Want It But 57% Have No Plan

**60%** of UK adults actively try to eat healthy (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). In the same survey, **57%** say they follow no nutrition rules at all (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). Those two numbers sit in the same dataset, from the same respondents. That is not a contradiction. It is a market.

The gap between wanting to eat well and having a system to do it is the single most important structural fact in this report. It tells operators that demand is not the problem. Structure is the problem. And structure is exactly what a fitness business sells.

Look at the dietary segments that do exist. **13%** follow a flexitarian diet. 12% follow low-carb or no-carb. 8% are lactose-free. 7% are gluten-free. 7% are vegetarian (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). Add those up and you reach 47%. But those segments overlap, and the headline remains: the majority consumer has no protocol. They are not ideologically committed to a tribe. They have not rejected structure. They simply have not been given one that works for them.

The flexitarian data makes this more pointed. Chart 5 shows the share of GB adults following a flexitarian diet from July 2019 to January 2024. The range is **13% to 16%** across the entire period (YouGov, July 2019 to January 2024). Five years. No meaningful movement. The category that was supposed to be the gateway to dietary structure has flatlined. Without an external mechanism providing accountability, education and habit reinforcement, consumers default to eating however they eat. That mechanism does not have to be a dietitian. It does not have to be a GP. It can be a gym.

This is a positioning argument with commercial teeth. The fitness operator who builds a credible nutrition layer into their offer is not competing with MyFitnessPal. They are filling a void that neither the NHS, nor the food industry, nor the wellness app market has closed. The member who walks in wanting to eat better but following no rules is not a hard sell. They are a waiting customer. The operator's job is to make the ask.

The 57% without a plan are not uniformly low-value clients. Many of them are paying full membership fees and attending regularly. Their nutrition is simply not connected to what they do in the gym. That disconnection is a missed commercial opportunity every month it continues. A nutrition add-on sold to 20% of an existing membership base, at even a modest monthly price, is a material revenue line. The intent is already there. The operator needs to supply the system.

## Cost of Living Is Now a Nutrition Competitor

**28%** of UK consumers said they were eating less healthily specifically to save money in 2023 (BBC; Censuswide; The Guardian, August 2023). **19%** said they were eating more ready meals and processed food for the same reason. These are not fringe responses. They represent tens of millions of adults choosing cheaper, lower-quality food because the alternative feels financially out of reach.

Chart 7 carries the full distribution of cost-of-living impacts on eating behaviour. The figures are unambiguous. Cost pressure is actively reversing the nutrition habits of a significant portion of the population. For fitness operators, this matters in two specific ways.

First, **65%** of UK consumers cite food prices as their top food-related concern, ahead of food waste at 62% and food quality at 61% (Food Standards Agency; Ipsos, October 2022 to January 2023). That ranking is significant. Quality ,  the thing the fitness industry tends to lead with ,  is not the primary concern. Price is. Any nutrition communication from an operator that ignores this is talking past the audience.

Second, **14%** of UK adults say they cannot afford to eat healthily (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). That is one in seven people. In a gym or studio context, that is a material share of the membership. When that person is considering whether to buy a nutrition coaching add-on, their primary objection is not scepticism about the content. It is that they have already decided that eating well is expensive. The operator who never addresses that belief will never sell to that person.

The commercial move here is not discounting. It is reframing. A 'high-performance eating on a real budget' offer ,  delivered through content, workshops, onboarding sessions, or a dedicated coaching track ,  turns an economic headwind into a loyalty driver. The member who learns from their gym how to eat better without spending more is not going to cancel their membership when times are hard. That gym is now solving a problem that is genuinely affecting their quality of life.

The 17% who said cost of ingredients was making them cook less, and the 15% who said cost-of-living stress was making them eat less healthily, are also worth noting (BBC; Censuswide; The Guardian, August 2023). Stress-driven eating and cooking avoidance are not nutrition problems at the surface level. They are behaviour problems. Coaches are better placed to address those than any food product or app.

Operators who treat cost-of-living as purely a threat to their own pricing power are missing the point. It is also a threat to their members' nutrition outcomes. And that makes it the operator's problem to solve, because member outcomes drive retention.

## The Generation Map: Who Is Willing to Pay and Who Needs the Most Help

**65%** of baby boomers actively try to eat healthy ,  the highest rate of any generation (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2022 to March 2023). Chart 6 shows this distribution clearly. Gen Z sits at 57%, Millennials at 62%, Gen X at 62%, and boomers at 65%. The gradient is modest but consistent: health motivation increases with age.

Now cross-reference food intolerance burden. **16%** of Gen Z report one or more food intolerances (Statista Consumer Insights, October 2022 to September 2023). That drops to 14% for Millennials, 12% for Gen X, and 8% for baby boomers. The oldest cohort has the highest motivation and the lowest dietary complexity. They are the easiest clients to programme for and the most likely to engage with a health-framed offer.

They are also the most neglected cohort in fitness industry product design. Studio concepts, marketing, class formats and coaching content all skew young. The boomer member who shows up with strong intent, relatively simple dietary needs and, in many cases, greater financial resilience than younger members is routinely underserved. That is a gap with a direct commercial correction available.

Gen Z presents the inverse profile. Highest intolerance complexity at 16%. Highest vegetarian adoption in the 18-19 age bracket at **11%** (Statista Consumer Insights, January to December 2023). Vegan adoption in the same cohort at 4% (Statista Consumer Insights, October 2022 to September 2023). These clients need more nuanced programming and coaches who understand restriction stacking ,  the challenge of managing a vegetarian or vegan diet alongside a food intolerance or an intolerance alongside an athletic performance goal.

Millennials and Gen X both sit at 62% for healthy eating intent. This is the core demographic for most studios and gyms ,  aged broadly 25 to 55. Strong motivation, but motivation alone does not drive behaviour change and it certainly does not drive membership upgrades. The commercial challenge with this group is conversion: from intent to structure, and from standard membership to a coached or enhanced nutrition offer.

Gender splits add another layer of programming specificity. **33%** of women say they are trying to eat less meat, compared to **26%** of men (Statista Consumer Insights, October 2022 to September 2023). Chart 10 shows this and the other gender-based differences. Men are significantly more open to cultured meat at **22%**, compared to 13% of women. Neither figure is close to mainstream adoption, but the directional difference matters. A nutrition coaching conversation with a male member who is open to novel proteins is a different conversation than one with a female member who is reducing meat for ethical or environmental reasons. Coaches who treat the whole membership as one dietary archetype are leaving precision on the table.

The generation map is not an academic exercise. It is a segmentation tool. Different generations require different outreach, different content, different pricing and different programming depth. Operators who treat nutrition as a single-track offer will serve some members adequately and most members poorly.

## The Plant-Based Reality Check: What the Market Data Actually Shows

The plant-based narrative of the early 2020s was built on a growth curve that did not continue. Chart 2 shows UK meat substitute market revenue from 2019 to 2029. Revenue peaked at **$715 million** in 2021, fell to $691 million in 2022, and as of 2023 had recovered only to $697 million (Statista Consumer Market Insights). The forecast to 2029 reaches $824 million ,  that is growth from the trough, but the 2021 peak has not been recovered by 2023 and the trajectory to recovery is shallow.

Per-capita consumption tells the same story with more precision. Chart 4 shows this clearly. UK consumers ate **1.05 kg** of meat substitutes per person in 2021. By 2023 that had fallen to 0.90 kg. The forecast from 2024 through 2029 is flat at 0.91 kg (Statista Consumer Market Insights). The category is not collapsing. But it is not growing at the household level. The volume increase in market totals between 2024 and 2029 reflects population size, not increased individual consumption.

Milk substitutes are a different story entirely. Chart 1 shows UK milk substitute market revenue from 2019 to 2029. Revenue grew from **$0.17 billion** in 2019 to $0.60 billion in 2023, and is projected to reach $1.12 billion by 2029 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). That is a 3.5x increase from 2019 to 2023. Per-capita consumption of milk substitutes rose from **1.56 litres** in 2019 to 3.16 litres in 2023 and is projected to reach 4.93 litres by 2029 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). The growth is real and it is at the individual level.

Chart 3 in the page's visual set covers UK vitamins and minerals retail sales, which we address in the next section, but the volume data for milk substitutes in the UK (Statista Consumer Market Insights) confirms that the market grew from 104 million kilograms in 2019 to 213.9 million kilograms in 2023 ,  and is projected to reach 319.17 million kilograms by 2028. That is structural, not cyclical, growth.

The IMARC Group projects the broader UK plant-based food market at **USD 1,084.4 million** by 2034, growing at a 10.41% CAGR from USD 433.1 million in 2025 (IMARC Group, UK Plant-Based Food Market Report 2034). That growth is real. But the composition of that growth matters. It is concentrated in beverages and dairy alternatives ,  oat milk, soy milk, plant-based yogurts and creams ,  not in burger patties and sausage substitutes.

For operators, this distinction has practical consequences. Stocking or recommending plant-based products as part of a nutrition programme should reflect where actual consumption is going, not where it was hyped to go. Milk alternatives in coffee, smoothies and cooking are now mainstream. Plant-based meat is a considered choice for a smaller segment. Programme design and product partnerships should reflect that split.

The UK's vegetarian food and drink sales data reinforces this. Revenue from vegetarian and plant-based alternatives reached £1,490 million in 2020, then fell to £1,085 million in 2021 and partially recovered to £1,158 million in 2022 (Ethical Consumer; The Co-operative Bank). The 2020 spike likely reflects pandemic-era pantry stocking and media attention. The correction was sharp. Operators and brands who built strategy on the 2020 figure were exposed.

The honest read of plant-based in the UK is this: dairy alternatives are a growing, mainstream category. Plant-based meat is a plateau. The fitness industry should engage with the former confidently and with the latter selectively, based on what their specific members actually consume.

## Supplements, Vitamins and the Retail Revenue Line Operators Are Leaving on the Table

**£492 million.** That is what UK consumers spent on vitamins and minerals through retail in 2023 (NielsenIQ; Proprietary Association of Great Britain). Chart 3 traces the trajectory from £324 million in 2009 to that 2023 figure ,  a 52% increase over fourteen years. The growth was not linear. Sales spiked **14.3%** in 2020 (NielsenIQ; Proprietary Association of Great Britain), a pandemic-driven surge in immune-support purchasing that did not fully unwind. By 2023, the market had settled above pre-pandemic levels and was growing again, up 6% on 2022.

The global context matters. The UK vitamins and minerals market generated **USD 723.66 million** in 2023, placing the UK seventh globally ,  ahead of Indonesia at $677 million and South Korea at $666 million (Statista Consumer Market Insights). The UK is a top-ten global market for this category. Its consumers are already buyers. The question for fitness operators is whether their members are buying from a supermarket, a pharmacy or from their gym.

The answer, in almost every case, is the supermarket or the pharmacy. Operators have largely ceded this retail category by default rather than by deliberate strategy. The member who asks their coach which protein powder to take, which vitamin D dose to consider, or whether probiotics are worth trying is a warm lead for a retail sale that currently goes elsewhere.

The UK probiotic supplements market adds urgency to this point. The market is projected to grow from **USD 77.2 million** in 2026 to USD 117.1 million in 2031 at an 8.7% CAGR (MarketsandMarkets, UK Probiotic Supplements Market 2026-2031). The driver is preventive health demand. That is exactly the framing a fitness coach uses every day. The member who is in a studio three times a week for performance and health outcomes is a natural buyer of a gut-health product if the recommendation comes from someone they trust.

The UK sports nutrition market sits at **USD 213.08 million** in 2025 and is projected to reach USD 357.81 million by 2035 at a 5.32% CAGR (Expert Market Research, UK Sports Nutrition Market 2025-2035). This is a category defined by the fitness consumer. It grows when coached activity grows. It is bought by people who are already members. The operator who carries no sports nutrition retail is watching that revenue leave their building with every member who walks out after a session.

The UK human microbiome market adds a forward-looking signal. Projected at **USD 72.0 million** in 2025, it is forecast to reach USD 334.4 million by 2030 at a 29.2% CAGR (MarketsandMarkets, UK Human Microbiome Market 2025-2030). This is early-stage science meeting mainstream health interest. Coaches who understand the gut-performance connection are positioning themselves ahead of a consumer education curve that is already moving.

Protein concentrate manufacturing data from the Office for National Statistics offers one more anchor. UK protein concentrate sales value reached **£529 million** in 2019 (the last clean year before data gaps), up from £147 million in 2008 (ONS, 2008 to 2022). This is a domestic supply industry that has grown fourfold in eleven years. The demand that drives it is sitting in gyms and studios across the country.

The retail move for an operator does not require significant capital. A curated range of five to ten products ,  protein, vitamin D, magnesium, a probiotic, an electrolyte ,  endorsed by coaches and connected to specific programming goals, is a meaningful margin line. The trust asymmetry between a coach's recommendation and a shelf display is enormous. That asymmetry has monetary value and most operators are not monetising it.

## Organic and Clean-Label: A £3.7 Billion Signal Operators Should Not Ignore

**£3.7 billion.** That is what UK consumers spent on organic food and drink through retail in 2024, up 7.3% year-on-year and more than double the £1.86 billion recorded in 2014 (Soil Association Certification, Organic Market Report 2025). Ten years. More than 2x growth. This is not a niche. It is the largest segment of the premium food market and it is growing faster than the broader food sector.

The label data from YouGov in 2022 tells the same story from a different angle. **31%** of UK grocery shoppers check free-range labels when buying food. 27% check low-sugar labels. 24% check animal welfare labels. 21% check environmentally friendly labels (YouGov, July 2022). These behaviours exist across the general population, not just among committed health consumers. They represent a widely distributed clean-label posture.

Cross-reference that with the attitudes data. **27%** of UK adults say they avoid artificial flavours and preservatives (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). That is more than one in four people. Many of them have no formal dietary system ,  they are in the 57% with no nutrition rules ,  but they are already making value-driven decisions at the supermarket shelf. They know what they do not want. They are looking for someone to tell them what to choose.

That is a coaching opportunity disguised as a grocery habit.

A fitness operator who trains their coaches to speak the same clean-label language that members already use in a supermarket aisle creates continuity between two parts of a member's life that currently have no connection. The member who reads the ingredient list on a protein bar before buying it is expressing a preference that their coach should know about and can build on. The coach who recommends products that match free-range, low-sugar and clean-ingredient standards is reducing the friction of every nutrition recommendation they make.

For retail and affiliate programmes, the alignment is direct. Members already have a revealed preference for clean-label products. Stocking products that match that preference, or building affiliate relationships with brands that hold those credentials, is meeting the member where they already are. The persuasion cost is low because the value system is shared.

The gluten-free food market in the UK offers a specific growth signal. The market is projected to grow by **USD 323.6 million** in absolute terms between 2025 and 2030 at a 9.1% CAGR (TechNavio, Gluten Free Food Market in UK 2026-2030). 7% of UK adults already follow gluten-free nutrition rules (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). In the Gen Z cohort, intolerance rates are highest, making gluten-free programming a real need rather than an optional niche for operators serving younger members.

The broader UK healthy food market is projected at **USD 76 billion** by 2035 at a 5.21% CAGR from a 2025 base (Market Research Future, UK Healthy Food Market 2035). That projection encompasses everything from organic produce to functional snacks to fortified beverages. The direction is unambiguous. The consumer is moving toward cleaner, more intentional food. The question for operators is whether they are visible in that movement or invisible to it.

## The Sweetener Shift: Sugar Is Losing Volume, Premium Alternatives Are Gaining Value

UK sugar consumption fell from **128.68 million kg** in 2018 to 119.65 million kg in 2022 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). The forecast through 2028 shows stabilisation around 123 million kg ,  not a collapse, but a clear plateau after years of decline. Per-capita consumption of sugar has followed the same pattern, falling from 1.87 kg in 2019 to 1.77 kg in 2022 and holding roughly flat through the forecast period (Statista Consumer Market Insights).

This is a slow structural shift, not a sudden behaviour change. Consumers are not eliminating sugar. They are reducing it gradually and substituting partially with alternatives. The soft drinks data shows where that substitution is most complete.

**66.3%** of carbonated soft drink market share in 2022 belonged to low and no calorie variants (Canadean; Kantar Worldpanel, 2022). For dilutable soft drinks, **89.3%** of market share sat with low and no calorie variants (Canadean; Kantar Worldpanel, 2022). In that category, regular-calorie products have essentially been displaced. These are not marginal numbers. They show that the sweetener shift in beverages is already the mainstream position, not a premium minority.

Artificial sweeteners by unit price tell the premiumisation story. The price per unit of artificial sweeteners in the UK stood at **$23.26** in 2022 and is forecast to rise to $28.29 by 2029 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). From 2022 to 2029, that is a 21.6% increase in price per unit. Better-for-you sweeteners are not getting cheaper as they scale. They are getting more expensive, which signals that demand is outpacing the commodity dynamic and that quality differentiation is driving value.

Honey shows a different but complementary dynamic. Honey revenue is growing faster than sugar in percentage terms in every year from 2022 through 2029 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). In 2022, honey revenue grew 9.39% year-on-year while sugar grew 6.06%. In 2023, honey grew 9.01% while sugar grew 6.27%. The volume story is smaller ,  honey consumption in the UK was 3.83 million kg in 2022 compared to 119.65 million kg for sugar ,  but the growth rate signals a consumer migration toward naturalness-coded alternatives. Honey is not a low-calorie product. It is a perceived-quality product. Consumers are choosing it because it feels less processed, not because it is better on a calorie-per-gram basis.

For fitness operators, the sweetener shift has two practical implications. First, the beverages sold in a gym retail unit or café should already be predominantly low or no calorie. The market has made this decision for mainstream consumers. Stocking regular-calorie sugary drinks in a fitness environment is actively contrary to where 66.3% of the carbonated drinks market already sits. Second, the clean-label preference for naturalness ,  honey over artificial sweetener, for many members ,  is a real factor in product selection. Both preferences can coexist in a product range if the operator understands the segment.

The coaching implication is direct. Sugar reduction is already happening in the UK population without coaching. Coaches who understand the substitution landscape ,  which sweeteners are neutral for performance, which are not, when honey is a useful frame and when it is not ,  are equipped to have evidence-based conversations that the member cannot get elsewhere.

## What Investors and Brands Should Read Into This

The intent-action gap is the most important structural signal in this dataset for capital allocation. **60%** of UK adults want to eat healthy and **57%** have no plan to do so (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). That gap has not closed. Flexitarian adoption has moved between 13% and 16% for five consecutive years without a durable increase (YouGov, July 2019 to January 2024). The implication is that product-layer interventions ,  better food products, more available alternatives, cleaner labels ,  are not sufficient to convert intent into consistent behaviour. The missing layer is behaviour change infrastructure: coaching, accountability, structured programming, habit reinforcement. Capital directed at that layer has a clearer demand signal than capital directed at another food product.

Platforms that turn attitude into tracked behaviour ,  whether that is a coaching app, a content subscription, a studio membership with embedded nutrition tracking, or an AI-assisted meal planning tool tied to training load ,  are addressing a documented gap. The fitness operator who builds that infrastructure at the local level is doing the same thing at smaller scale with higher trust.

Plant-based meat is a category in correction and investors pricing in 2021-era assumptions face model risk. Revenue peaked at **$715 million** in 2021 and had reached only $697 million by 2023 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). Per-capita consumption peaked at 1.05 kg in 2021, fell to 0.90 kg by 2023, and is forecast flat at 0.91 kg from 2024 through 2029 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). The category is not terminal ,  $824 million in revenue by 2029 is a real market ,  but the volume story at the individual consumer level has stopped growing. Brands and investors building on a volume-growth thesis need to check that thesis against Chart 4.

The better alternative protein bet is dairy. Milk substitute market revenue grew from **$0.17 billion** in 2019 to $0.60 billion in 2023 and is projected to reach $1.12 billion by 2029 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). Volume grew from 104 million kg in 2019 to 213.9 million kg in 2023 and is projected to reach 319.17 million kg by 2028 (Statista Consumer Market Insights). Per-capita consumption is growing every year to 2029. This is a category with durable individual-level demand growth, not just population-scale expansion.

The sports nutrition market offers defensible growth for buyers and sponsors in the fitness channel. At a **5.32% CAGR** from USD 213.08 million in 2025 to USD 357.81 million by 2035 (Expert Market Research, UK Sports Nutrition Market 2025-2035), it grows steadily and it grows with the fitness industry. The probiotic supplements market at **8.7% CAGR** from USD 77.2 million in 2026 to USD 117.1 million by 2031 (MarketsandMarkets, UK Probiotic Supplements Market 2026-2031) adds a fast-growing adjacent category. Both categories should be weighted more heavily than broader plant-based in a fitness-channel portfolio.

The macro growth picture is real but the cost-of-living drag is a constraint on mass-market pricing. The **£3.7 billion** organic market growing at 7.3% (Soil Association Certification, Organic Market Report 2025) and the UK healthy food market projected at **USD 76 billion** by 2035 (Market Research Future, UK Healthy Food Market 2035) confirm that the premium health consumer is spending more and will continue to. But **28%** of consumers are eating less healthily to save money (BBC; Censuswide; The Guardian, August 2023). Mass-market brands that price and position exclusively for the premium segment are ignoring a quarter of the population that is actively moving in the wrong direction on nutrition. Value narratives are not a retreat from brand quality. They are a requirement for market scale.

The most underpriced commercial asset in this data is the trust relationship between a fitness coach and their members. A coach's product recommendation carries a weight that no supermarket shelf or social media ad can replicate. Acquisition-minded supplement and functional food brands have largely treated the fitness channel as a sampling and sampling-only opportunity. The smarter play is deeper commercial integration: structured affiliate programmes, co-branded products, exclusive ranges and revenue-sharing on retail sales. The trust asymmetry is the asset. It has not been priced properly.

## Six Moves Operators and Coaches Should Make Now

Map your membership against the five dietary segments from Chart 8: flexitarian at **13%**, low-carb at 12%, lactose-free at 8%, gluten-free at 7%, vegetarian at 7% (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). Most studios have no idea what their members actually eat. A simple intake form at onboarding, or a member survey, gives you the data you need to design programming, stock products and personalise coaching conversations. Without that data, you are making assumptions about a population that has meaningfully different dietary needs at the individual level.

Build a two-track nutrition offer. The **43%** of members who already follow some form of dietary structure are candidates for a premium coached nutrition protocol ,  higher-touch, more personalised, more accountable, priced accordingly. The **57%** who follow no rules are the larger group and they need an entry-level education track: simple content, basic habit frameworks, low-commitment starting points (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). The entry-level track converts to the premium track over time. It is a pipeline, not a charity service.

Train coaches to speak the retail label language that members already use. **31%** of members check free-range labels. **27%** check low-sugar. **24%** check animal welfare (YouGov, July 2022). These are the vocabulary of your members' grocery decisions. A coach who can connect a training goal to a shopping habit ,  "when you're buying protein, here's what to look for on the label" ,  is delivering practical value that the member cannot find anywhere else in their fitness relationship.

Open a vitamins, minerals or sports nutrition retail line. The UK market is **£492 million** and growing (NielsenIQ; Proprietary Association of Great Britain, 2023). Start small: five to eight SKUs that coaches genuinely use and recommend. Vitamin D, magnesium, a clean protein, an electrolyte, a probiotic. Price them fairly. Let coaches explain the why behind each product. Even a modest retail margin across a medium-sized membership base is meaningful. The member would spend that money elsewhere without the guidance. The guidance is the differentiator.

Address cost-of-living directly in your content and onboarding. **28%** of consumers are eating less healthily to save money and **14%** say they cannot afford to eat healthily (BBC; Censuswide; The Guardian, August 2023; Statista Consumer Insights, April 2023 to March 2024). These members are at the highest churn risk. They are the ones who disengage from nutrition goals first when financial pressure rises, and disengage from membership soon after. A monthly 'healthy eating on a real budget' content piece ,  recipe ideas, shopping list breakdowns, cost-per-gram protein comparisons ,  costs almost nothing to produce. It directly counters the primary reason members disengage from nutrition goals. It is also a powerful acquisition tool because it demonstrates that your coaching is practical, not aspirational.

Target and programme specifically for the boomer member. At **65%** health motivation (Statista Consumer Insights, April 2022 to March 2023), **8%** food intolerance complexity (Statista Consumer Insights, October 2022 to September 2023) and greater average financial resilience than younger cohorts, this is the most commercially attractive and most neglected segment in fitness industry product design. Build a class format, a nutrition track and a coaching offer that speaks directly to a 60-year-old's goals: energy, longevity, joint health, metabolic function. Market it without youth-coded language or imagery. The demand is documented. The supply is almost non-existent.

**Three things to do this month.** First, add a dietary profile question to your member onboarding or send a one-question survey to your existing membership asking which of the eight dietary categories from Chart 8 best describes them ,  including 'none'. The data will surprise you. Second, identify one cost-of-living nutrition topic ,  batch cooking, protein-per-pound cost ranking, cheap anti-inflammatory foods ,  and publish one piece of content on it before the end of the month. Third, contact one supplement or functional food brand whose products your coaches already use personally and ask what a formal affiliate or retail arrangement would look like. The conversation costs nothing and the upside is a recurring revenue line that requires no additional session hours.
